## What the source is about

This is a labor history of the people who worked directly for the VOC and the EIC in Bengal before the Battle of Plassey in 1757. It covers "boatmen, cartmen, coolies, silk reelers, slaves, sailors and soldiers" (p. iv [PDF 4]). It compares the two companies side by side, which is exactly what I need. Her main argument is that workers had a lot of power because they could run away, and the companies could not fully control them: "the European companies' control over the workers was far from total." (p. 1 [PDF 14]) But the book is also full of violence: slavery in company households, executions of deserters, flogging, and kidnapping of workers. That makes it my main source for the ethics section. It is not about share prices at all.

## Facts I can use

### Background: how big each company's Bengal trade was
- VOC trade from Bengal rose "from 150,534 florins in 1648-49 to 4,615,986 florins in 1720-1721." (p. 29 [PDF 42])
- EIC: "From the early eighteenth century, the EIC's total trade value from Bengal to Europe was marginally higher than VOC's." By 1750 it was "511, 177 pounds, representing more than half (50.4 percent) of the EIC's total trade from Asia." (p. 29 [PDF 42], citing Chaudhuri)
- The VOC lost the European silk market to the EIC. VOC raw silk imports fell to 88,000 ponds by 1739, "because by this time, the EIC had, through its re-export trade, taken over much of the European market for Eastern textiles." (p. 31 [PDF 44])
- But "the VOC's trade in saltpeter dominated that of the EIC until the 1750s." (p. 31 [PDF 44])
- What this means for me: in my 1725-1750 window the EIC was growing faster in Bengal. This supports picking the EIC.

### Slavery: the VOC
- Officially the VOC was against it. It called it the "abominable trade" and in 1732 issued statutes banning Patna fleet crews from capturing Bengalis to sell. (p. 237 [PDF 250])
- But: "although the VOC as a corporate entity did not participate in the slave trade, many of their employees, including the directors, did." There is evidence of "public auctions" for slaves in VOC settlements. (p. 237-238 [PDF 250-251])
- She rebuilt 78 slave sales from the Dutch notarial records. Slaves came from Malabar, Coromandel, Makassar, Sulawesi, Padang, Colombo and Batavia. (p. 238 [PDF 251]) "the VOC was the only corporate trading entity in the Indian Ocean which could integrate so many places into one complex slave trade network." (p. 238 [PDF 251])
- Appendix A lists dozens of sales in 1732-1743 (p. 294-296 [PDF 307-309]). Ship officers used slaves as a side income: "Evidently, the slaves were supplementing the wages of these officers." (p. 239 [PDF 252])
- The VOC director Kerseboom freed 45 slaves when he left in 1755. Director Cornelis de Jonge freed five slave families when he left in 1743. (p. 243 [PDF 256]; p. 250 [PDF 263])
- So the top VOC officials in Bengal during my investment period owned dozens of slaves.

### Slavery: the EIC
- The EIC side has fewer records, but she says: "There are also strong indications that the EIC, and not simply its officials in private capacities, was involved in the overseas slave trade." (p. 241 [PDF 254])
- In 1706-07 the Fort William council ordered slaves bought to ship to York Fort (Sumatra). The order says to "take care yt[sic] that they are most men and boys and few women and girls, and see they are sound, wholesome and well shaped when bought." (p. 242 [PDF 255])
- Calcutta had "regular collection of duties on the sale of slaves in Calcutta markets." (p. 241 [PDF 254]) So the EIC taxed slave sales.
- November 1742: "Manuel Joseph, an EIC soldier, was apprehended in Decca with twelve slaves." (p. 241 [PDF 254])

### Slavery: both companies
- "Almost all male company servants, from the common soldier to the Director, owned slaves." (p. 291 [PDF 304])
- Female slaves were sexually exploited: "Female slaves in the settlements often performed sexual services." (p. 246 [PDF 259])

### Violence and punishment of slaves and servants
- VOC, 1736: a slave named Ganoe killed his master and was sentenced "to death on a breaking wheel, after strips of flesh were cut off of his body with a hot iron," and his body was left at the gate "as a warning to the slave population." (p. 274 [PDF 287])
- VOC, 1739: September van Mandhaar killed his master and "was apprehended and sentenced to the rack." (p. 271 [PDF 284])
- VOC, 1729: two runaway slaves and a mardijker were "whipped mercilessly and sentenced to twenty-five years of hard labor in Batavia." (p. 275 [PDF 288])
- EIC Calcutta, 1728: an enslaved woman, Hannah, who stole and ran away with her lover was sentenced to death by the Mayor's Court even though she was pregnant. (p. 272-273 [PDF 285-286]) (p. 275 [PDF 288])
- EIC Calcutta, 1719: two EIC ship mates raped a nine-year-old slave girl and a five-year-old girl, and got only "thirty-nine lashes," while four men convicted of burglary "were all given death sentences." (p. 269-270 [PDF 282-283])
- Beating servants was normal: "beating one's own servant was deemed only natural." (p. 269 [PDF 282])

### Treatment of European sailors and soldiers
- Wages: EIC common soldiers got "a monthly wage of Rs. 10," and "Rs. 5 for the Portuguese/Black soldier on the bottom-most rung." (p. 160 [PDF 173]) VOC common soldiers got between 9 and 14 guilders. (p. 161 [PDF 174])
- Debt trap in the VOC: some had debts of over 100 guilders, "and with a wage of just 9 guilders these workers were condemned to endless wage slavery for the company." (p. 173 [PDF 186])
- In 1734 Dutch sailors who tried to run said they were treated "not as company servants but as slaves." Three were executed. (p. 152-153 [PDF 165-166])
- "twenty-one death penalties were given for desertion by fiscaal in the Bengal directorate, of which seventeen were carried out in Chinsura." (p. 171 [PDF 184]) Also flogging, running the gauntlet, and "three to twenty-five years of hard labor in chains." (p. 170 [PDF 183])
- 1734: a VOC deserter was hanged and his corpse "cut into pieces and fed to the fish." (p. 194 [PDF 207])
- VOC desertion was huge: "In certain years, desertion rose to a staggering one third to one half of the total number of European servants of the VOC in the Bengal directorate." (p. 172-173 [PDF 185-186])
- The EIC handled it differently. In 1744 when soldiers complained about pay, "the EIC decided to increase their diet money and advance them half a month's wages rather than risk losing them to desertion." (p. 188 [PDF 201]) The EIC actually filled its army with VOC runaways. (p. 188 [PDF 201])
- "By the middle of the eighteenth century, the wages of the European sailors in the EIC far surpassed those in the VOC." (p. 197 [PDF 210])
- Note: she says the VOC archive is just "better and denser" for desertion (p. 157 [PDF 170]), so the VOC may look harsher partly because it kept better records.

### Indian wage workers, silk reelers, and forced labor
- Rowers earned about 1 anna a day (p. 290 [PDF 303]). VOC peons recruiting workers were paid "a meager wage of 1 anna/day." (p. 110 [PDF 123])
- VOC silk reelers were flogged. In 1662 the master reeler complained "the flog has been used too much." The VOC also planned foot soldiers "to hunt down deserting reelers." (p. 221 [PDF 234]) This is from before my period.
- EIC 1758: "they sent in the army to kidnap head reelers and common reelers and kept them under arrest in their own residence in Kassimbazar." (p. 227 [PDF 240]) "Coercion was central to the EIC's strategy." (p. 223 [PDF 236]) Just after my selling window, but it shows where the EIC was going.
- But she also says that most of the time workers had bargaining power and "the companies usually had to comply with the demands of the workers." (p. 289 [PDF 302])

### The 1740s: Maratha raids and war
- "In the midst of the Maratha attacks in the 1740s, there was a shortage in the labor force as people were fleeing their villages in hundreds. In such a situation, all the companies were impressing workers." (p. 110 [PDF 123]) So both companies used forced recruitment in the 1740s.
- "Displacement caused by Maratha invasions likely brought down wages." In 1744 rowers got 18 annas a month. (p. 106 [PDF 119])
- Slave sales from Bengal "rose significantly in the 1750s, possibly due to depredations brought about by the Marathas in the previous decade." (p. 240 [PDF 253])
- Desertion to the old Ostend Company base at Bankibazar shot up again in 1744, until Alivardi Khan destroyed it in 1745 "on the pretext that the Ostenders were abetting the Marathas." She says "around three hundred sailors of the EIC deserted to Bankibazaar during this period." (p. 184 [PDF 197])

## Gaps
- Nothing on share prices, dividends, or profits for investors. I need other sources for that.
- Only covers Bengal. Nothing on Batavia (1740 massacre), Ceylon, or the VOC spice islands, which is where VOC slavery and forced labor were much bigger. She even says VOC slave holdings in Asia were "much larger than Dutch imperial slave holdings in the Atlantic" (p. 235 [PDF 248]) but doesn't go into it.
- Nothing about Madras 1746 or the War of the Austrian Succession.
- EIC evidence is thinner than VOC evidence, so the comparison is a bit lopsided.

## How useful is this source? (my take)
Very useful for ethics, not useful for money. It is the best thing I have that compares the EIC and VOC as employers in the same place and time, with real cases and dates from 1725-1750. My takeaway: both companies were deeply tied to slavery and violence, so an ethical investor would not have a clean choice. The VOC looks worse in Bengal on punishment (executions, torture, breaking wheel) and had the bigger slave network across Asia. The EIC looks a little softer toward its own European workers in the 1740s (raised pay instead of hanging), but it bought slaves as a company in 1706, taxed slave sales, and by 1758 was kidnapping workers by force. She is not trying to rank which company was worse, so that call is mine.
