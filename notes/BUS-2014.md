## What the source is about

The file is NOT the whole journal issue. It is the issue's contents page plus one article. The issue is a special edition in memory of Professor John Tiley, and most of the other articles (listed on the contents page) are about UK, US and Australian tax. The article in the file is the last one, about how the VOC "farmed out" taxes in Malacca. Tax farming means the VOC auctioned the right to collect a tax to a private person, who paid the VOC up front and then collected what he could from taxpayers (p. 254, p. 269).

The paper covers the whole 1700s but its detailed data is mostly 1770 to 1790 (p. 255). Still, it has some useful facts for 1725 to 1750: Malacca was losing money, the VOC was described as "in decline" in 1741, and the VOC changed its tax system in 1743 to 1745. It also has material on coercion and slavery for the ethics part. It says nothing about the EIC as a company or about VOC share prices or dividends.

## Facts I can use

### Basic VOC background
- The VOC "was formed in 1602 in the Dutch Republic from a range of smaller trading companies." The States-General (Dutch parliament) granted its Charter, "which was renewed periodically." The Charter "permitted the VOC to enter into treaties with foreign powers." (p. 260-261)
- The company's "commercial purpose was to procure goods for resale in Europe." (p. 254)
- "Trade and tax were the two streams of VOC revenue." (p. 254) This is a key difference I can test against the EIC: the VOC was also acting like a government that taxed people.
- The VOC took Malacca from the Portuguese in 1641, which "entitled the Dutch to taxation rights, such as the collection of waterway tolls, and the opportunity to contract trade monopolies on key commodities, such as tin and pepper." (p. 254)
- The VOC "was finally declared bankrupt in 1798." (p. 261) Useful for the long run, but after my period.

### How much the VOC relied on tax
- Reid says "by 1653 the VOC's tax farms were a major source of revenue, representing 27% of its income from Asia." (p. 257)
- Footnote citing Gaastra: trade was "about 60%" of total VOC revenue for Asia in the 18th century. (p. 267, n. 74)
- In Malacca 1775 to 1790, tax and customs leases made up "a vital 44% of revenue." (p. 267) This is after my period.

### VOC fortunes during 1725 to 1750 (most useful part)
- Malacca lost money for decades: "the trend is of net losses averaging about 100,000 Dutch Guilders per annum for the years 1721 to 1769, under nine successive VOC Governors." (p. 265) So all through my investment period, this station was a drain.
- Batavia had to cover the losses with a "cross-subsidy" because the Straits of Malacca "needed to be kept open for VOC ships." (p. 265)
- **1741 Van Imhoff report:** "In a 1741 report by VOC Batavia Governor-General-designate, Baron Van Imhoff, the VOC was described as a company in decline, requiring drastic policy reforms." He found the VOC monopoly system "'ineffective, expensive and counter-productive'." (p. 265) His report was discussed by the Directors "but only small reforms were enacted." (p. 266) This is a big one for me. A top VOC insider was saying in 1741, halfway through my period, that the company was declining.
- One of those small reforms: from 1744 private tax farmers bid at auction for the customs farm (*boom pacht*). (p. 266) The first year's customs lease brought in 15,500 rijksdollars in 1744. (p. 266, n. 68)
- Java: in 1743 "the Dutch VOC assumed taxation privileges for Java's northeast coast after its subjugation of the Mataram," and held "its first tax farming auction in Batavia in 1743." (p. 256) So the VOC was expanding its tax income right as trade looked weak.
- Dutch officials at Malacca got "a quarter of the profits from customs duties, in accordance with their rank." (p. 266) "VOC officials had been allowed a percentage of customs duties since 1745." (p. 268) The authors think officials later took more than their share (p. 268). That suggests money leaking away from shareholders to employees.
- Tea sales figures from Glamann (footnote): "1698/1700: 4,100 Guilders; 1738/1740: 24,920 Guilders; 1778/1780: 22,920 Guilders." (p. 268, n. 78) Tea was growing fast up to 1740. The units look too small to be real totals, so I need to check Glamann before using these numbers.
- Governors of Malacca in my period (p. 263): Herman Van Suchtelen 1717-1727, Johan Gobius 1727-1730, Pieter De Chavonnes 1731-1735, Rogier De Laver 1736-1743, Willem Albinus 1743-1749, Pieter Van Heemskerk 1749-1753.
- Malacca Town population in 1750 was 9,635 (up from 4,884 in 1678). (p. 270, Figure 7)
- Small tax farm totals: 5,991 rijksdollars in 1742 and 5,834 in 1743 (Appendix A, p. 279).

### Ethics: coercion and slavery
- One thing VOC tax farming shared with older systems was "the use of an agent's physical coercion against taxpayers." (p. 256)
- In Java in the 1740s there were "claims of tax farmers overcharging and intimidating taxpayers." (p. 256)
- In Malacca (later period) "there were many accounts of bullying and oppressive tactics to extract taxes." (p. 268)
- Customs were discriminatory. The authors call it "'institutionally endorsed discrimination' against certain taxpayers." Chinese and non-Johor Malays paid more, while Dutch burghers "paid no tax." (p. 275) Groups not allied with the VOC "were supressed ... by the dominance of Dutch mercantilism and its practice of forced docking at VOC ports." (p. 276)
- Malay farmers had a tithe squeezed out of them by local headmen, who were "free to extract as much as he could from the cultivators." (p. 276)
- **Slavery:** in the 1668 customs rules "Slaves were also duty-free to provide much-needed labour." (p. 272) The customs table lists slaves duty free for the VOC and Johor nobles, 10 rijksdollars for others, with "slave children, 50% disc." (p. 281, Appendix C). This is 1668, not my period, but it shows the VOC treated enslaved people as taxable goods.
- The VOC did not have to spend any tax money on the community: "there was no requirement for the application of tax receipts to social welfare or infrastructure." (p. 263)

### Link to the EIC (small)
- Malacca's decline as a port began "from at least the 1780s with the rise of British trade dominance from bases in India." (p. 262) After my period.
- English ship arrivals at Malacca rose from 17 in 1761 to 56 in 1775 (p. 274, Figure 10). Also after my period.

## Gaps
- Almost nothing directly on 1725 to 1750 except the Malacca losses, Van Imhoff 1741, and the 1743 to 1745 tax changes. The detailed data is 1770 to 1790 (p. 255).
- Only one VOC port. Malacca losing money does not mean the whole VOC was.
- No share prices, dividends, or anything about the Amsterdam stock market.
- Nothing about the EIC as a business, so no comparison.
- Nothing on the 1740 Batavia massacre of the Chinese, even though it talks about Chinese tax farmers.
- Appendix A has a gap between 1743 and 1779 (p. 279).
