The Allen article is about how the EIC used slave labour at its pepper factory at Bencoolen (Fort Marlborough) on Sumatra. It uses EIC records from the India Office Records at the British Library. This is directly useful for the ethics part of my paper, and many of its facts fall inside 1725 to 1750.

## Facts I can use

### The EIC used and shipped slaves
- The EIC set up Bencoolen in July 1685 "as part of its plan to secure supplies of Sumatran pepper." (p. 153)
- The EIC moved there because of "the existence of a Dutch East India Company (Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie, or VOC) factory at Priaman and concern about Dutch plans to monopolize the pepper trade." (p. 153) Shows the two companies competing.
- "the EIC not only made use of chattel labor, but also shipped slaves thousands of miles to satisfy the demand for such labor at its various establishments." (p. 153)
- Numbers: "the company acquired at least 10,000 and perhaps as many as 15,000 to 20,000 African, Indian, Malagasy, and Southeast Asian slaves between the 1620s and late eighteenth century, mostly between the 1690s and early 1770s." (p. 153-154) So most of this was going on during my investment period.
- Comparison with the Dutch: "These numbers pale in comparison to the hundreds of thousands of slaves traded by the Dutch, French, Portuguese, and others in the Indian Ocean." (p. 154) Useful for comparing EIC and VOC ethics, but it lumps the Dutch in with others, so it is not a VOC only figure.
- Histories of the EIC "rarely mention this activity" because records from Bombay, Calcutta and Madras are scarce. (p. 154) So slavery was probably bigger than the records show.
- "Madagascar supplied the cargoes for 48 of the 75 known or authorized/probable company slaving voyages between 1622 and 1772." (p. 156)

### Why the EIC wanted slaves (profit motive)
- Officials said slaves "could be made to work at all hours and cost little or nothing to maintain." (p. 155)
- In 1704 they claimed slaves could do twice the work of Malay "coolies" and cost "no more than $14 a year each to maintain." Similar claims were made in 1719 and 1758. (p. 155)
- Racist rankings: "one Malagasy slave was worth two or three from India, three or four from Nias, and three Malays." (p. 156)

### Events inside 1725 to 1750
- Slave population at Bencoolen (Table 2, p. 158): 267 in 1725, 217 in 1727, 166 in 1730, 169 in 1733, 192 in 1737, 258 in 1742, and 214 or 237 in 1748 (two censuses).
- Slave origins in 1742: 226 Malagasy, 18 Malabar, 14 Nias, total 258. (Table 1, p. 157)
- Buying people: in 1731 officials bought "Andrew, a Malabar boatman, for $60" and in 1739 "paid $50 each for 12 'able stout men' from a group of 25 Nias slaves." (p. 157-158)
- Court of Directors letters in the 1730s and 1750s told officials to treat slaves "humanely." (p. 159) But the reason was money. The 1713 letter said: "they are likely to be more beneficial to our Affairs the longer they live." (p. 160) In March 1740 the Court ordered that Bencoolen's slaves "were to be employed constantly to the company's benefit." (p. 160)
- Craftsmen were "at least 25 percent and sometimes as much as 42 percent of 'effective' adult male slaves between 1730 and 1786." (p. 160)
- 1735: "a slave woman named Hannah was found guilty of poisoning the fort's armorer, a crime for which she was sentenced to be flogged 39 times at each of three places and then kept in irons." (p. 162)
- 1745: supplies from India might not arrive, which "led to a reduction in slaves' monthly rice allowance." (p. 162)
- 1745: Edward Hurlock reported four slaves missing at Moco Moco and worried about a village of runaway slaves near Palembang. (p. 163)
- Larger escapes "may also have occurred in 1727, 1737, 1739." (p. 163) Eight Malabars escaped in 1737 and were not caught. (p. 163)
- 1748: smallpox killed "a large number of slaves," and the directors ordered the ship *Swallow* "to Madagascar to purchase 250 slaves to replace those who had died." (p. 155) This is right in my 1745 to 1750 selling window. The directors in London were ordering a slaving voyage.
- Nias people bought the freedom of relatives and countrymen in 1732 ($50), 1735 ($25) and 1741 ($15). (p. 164)

### Violence and abuse (outside or around my period)
- 1711: Flyamango, a slave "who had been regularly abused and beaten severely, was beaten to death after a failed attempt to escape." (p. 162)
- 1721: "widespread sexual exploitation of women slaves." (p. 162)
- Slaves often ran "toward Dutch-controlled territory." (p. 163)

### Cost of slaves to the company
- In 1757, $520 a month on slaves was "22 percent of total monthly expenditures." (p. 165)
- In 1785, slave costs were $26,000, "more than 11 percent of the factory's annual budget." (p. 165)

### The VOC (brief)
- "The VOC had pioneered the use of such labor [convict labour] in the Indian Ocean world by shipping Ceylonese, Chinese, and Javanese prisoners to the Cape of Good Hope ... a practice that continued well into the eighteenth century." (p. 166-167)
- "the VOC's experience with slavery is a subject of ever greater scholarly interest ... many aspects of this activity remain hidden from view." (p. 168)

### Later (after 1750, only for context)
- Emancipation was proposed in 1786 but the Court withdrew support in May 1790 (p. 164-166). Slavery in EIC India territories was only abolished by "Act V of 1843." (p. 164)

## How useful is this source? (my take)

Useful, but only one article out of six. The Allen article is one of my best sources for the EIC side of the ethics question. It proves the EIC bought, shipped and punished enslaved people during 1725 to 1750, with specific dated examples (1731, 1735, 1739, 1740, 1745, 1748). The 1748 *Swallow* order is especially strong because it is in my selling window and came from the directors in London. Keep it in the bibliography, but cite it as the Allen article, not as the whole issue. It does not help with the non-English requirement because it is written in English, and the Spanish articles in the issue are irrelevant to my topic.
