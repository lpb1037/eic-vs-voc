## What the source is about

This article is about EIC *bonds*, not EIC *shares*. India bonds were short-term debts the East India Company issued in London. The authors show that in practice the bonds rolled over and acted like long-term bonds, where the Company could pay them off at par (a "call") and holders could cash them in at par (a "put"). Because of that, older historians like Dickson got the yields wrong and wrongly decided the London market was inefficient. The authors say London investors understood these bonds and priced them correctly. For my paper the main value is (1) a year by year table of EIC debt and loans to the government 1702-1753, (2) how the war years 1739-1746 hit British bond prices, and (3) how close the EIC was to the British state. There is nothing on the VOC.

## Facts I can use

### What an India bond was
- "In exchange for long-term loans, the government allowed the East India Company to issue short-term debentures, the India bonds, with coupons ranging from 3 to 6 per cent depending on the period." (p. 369)
- They were "nominally short-term debts, they were in practice rolled over and thus became equivalent to long-term bonds." (p. 371)
- A period trading manual said "the East India Company's Bonds are payable [by the Company] upon six Months Notice and the Company receives them as Cash in Payment for Goods bought at their Sales, when six Months Interest is due upon them" (Massie 1750, quoted p. 371).
- Each bond was usually £100. Mortimer (1761): "These bonds are usually for £100 each, and the seller receives the interest of the purchaser, up to the day he sells" (quoted p. 393, note 31).
- Prices stayed near par and never went below it. "the prices of India bonds remain very close to par, as an effect of the call option, but never drop below it, as an effect of the put option" (p. 385).
- So bonds vs stock: an EIC bond was a safe, cash-like asset. Massie (1750) said "they may be esteemed ready Cash running at Interest" (quoted p. 386). EIC stock, which paid dividends and moved with trade profits, is a different thing and this article does not cover it.

### EIC relationship to the government
- The bonds were "'secured' by debts due from the government to the Company and the East India Company was monitored by Parliament, which set the volume of debt authorised." (p. 373)
- Dickson quote: "a statute of 1721 empowered the East India Company to take up money on its common seal up to the sum which the government owed the company." (p. 373, note 10)
- The authors conclude "the risk on East India Company debts was the same as that on government debts over the whole period 1718-63." (p. 374) They also say "no major institutional change in the relations between the East India Company and the government occurred between 1718 and 1751" (p. 374).
- The Company "was highly renowned and on very close terms with the government. This made it as secure for investors as the government itself" (p. 388).
- Parliament limited what the EIC could borrow. "Parliament had forbidden the East India Company to issue long-term bonds in competition with the government's or shorter-term notes in competition with the Bank of England's." (p. 388)
- Dickson on 1749-1750: "The government, having run the company into debt by borrowing from it under duress, now used its permission to reduce the debt as a carrot to persuade the East India proprietors to agree (25 April 1750) to a general reduction of interest" (quoted p. 374, note 11). The words "under duress" are useful. The state was leaning on the Company.

- What I see: from 1725 to 1743 the EIC lent the government £3.2 million. In 1744 that jumps to £4.2 million, during the war. EIC bonds jump by about £700,000 the next year (1745). The article does not explain why the loan went up in 1744, so I need another source for that (probably a loan in return for extending the charter, but I have to check).
- The interest the government paid the EIC on its loan fell from 5% (1725) to 4% (1730) to 3% (1750) (p. 391-392). That is income lost to EIC shareholders.
- The EIC made money on the gap: in 1725 it got 5% from the government and paid 4% on bonds (p. 391). By 1737-1745 it was getting 4% and paying 3% (p. 391-392).
- In 1749 bonds "was £4,242,000, which roughly equalled the volume of loans to the government (£4,200,000). In 1752, one year after the Conversion, India bonds were reduced to £1,800,000, and the volume of India annuities equalled £3,000,000." (p. 388, note 25)

### Interest rate changes on the bonds
- The rate went to 4% in 1728 and 3% in 1737 per Dickson: "the yield fell abruptly in 1728, and again in 1737, when the nominal rate was reduced respectively to 4% and 3%" (quoted pp. 370-371).
- The 1732-33 cut from 4% to 3% "met with strong criticism from investors." (p. 383) Some holders refused and kept 3.5%, and the Company paid them off over time (p. 375, note 14).
- "From 1734 to 1743 (Figure 12), the price of the Consol fluctuates between 95 and 105" (p. 383). Calm years.

### The 1740s war years
- The authors split the time into "two periods of rising Consol prices (1728-39, 1746-53), and two periods of falling Consol prices (1739-46, 1753-9)." (p. 386) The fall from 1739 to 1746 lines up with the War of Jenkins' Ear and the War of the Austrian Succession, though the article never names the wars.
- "From 1744, the price of the Consol starts decreasing, and the price of the India bond also declines from close to 105 in 1743, to just above 100 in 1746. In May 1746, the coupon is raised from 3 to 4 per cent (at a price of the India bond of 100.85)." (p. 383) So in the war, the EIC had to pay more to borrow.
- **Primary source quote:** "The Course of the Exchange of 21 March 1746 simply indicates: 'The Court of Directors of the India Company have resolved to give 4 per cent. on their Bonds from the 31st Instant.'" (p. 383)
- "During the period 1746-9 (Figure 13), the price of the Consols remains below 90." (p. 384) Government debt was cheap in the late war years.
- After the war: "After Pelham's Conversion in April 1749 (consolidation of all government's floating debts), the price of the Consols rose very fast." (p. 384) "In May 1750, when the price of the Consol reaches 99.88, the coupon of the India bonds is reduced from 4 to 3 per cent." (p. 384)
- A pamphleteer in November 1749 said lowering interest on the National Debt "is now pretended to be more practicable, as means have been contrived to raise the three per Cents. above par, and to reduce India bonds from 4 to 3 per Cent." (quoted p. 384, note 21)

- During the war fall (1739-46) government stock lost money each year on average but India bonds still made 2.12% (p. 387). "India bonds' average returns are never negative when Consol prices fall" (p. 386).
- The authors warn this return number "is very sensitive to the periods chosen, as the difference between the buy and sell prices accounts for a large share" (p. 386). That matters for my "best year to sell" question.

### What this suggests about timing a sale (my reading, for bonds and general London prices)
- London prices of safe assets were lowest around 1746-1749 (Consols under 90, p. 384) and recovered fast after the 1748 peace and Pelham's conversion in 1749 (p. 384). If EIC stock followed the same pattern, selling in 1749-1750 would beat selling in 1745-1747. I must check this with real EIC share prices from Neal (1990) or the Course of the Exchange.

### The Course of the Exchange (primary source description)
- It was "a twice-weekly financial bulletin" with exchange rates and "the prices of the shares of the most important companies in the London Stock Exchange (mainly the Bank of England, East India Company and South Sea Company)". It was "created in the years 1696-7 by Jonathan Castaing" (p. 392).
- India bonds appear in "the fifth section of the bulletin, entitled 'Bonds'" and show the premium in pounds and shillings (p. 393).
- Copies are held in the "University of London. Library Collection and British Museum Collection. British Library (St Pancras)." (p. 391)
- The authors' monthly price data were posted as Excel at www.financesinternationales.sciences-po.fr/fr/bases.htm (p. 381, note 18). Might be dead now.

## How useful is this source? (my take)
Medium for my question. It does not tell me how EIC *shares* or the VOC did, so it cannot answer "which company" on its own. But it is a solid peer reviewed article with a real year by year table of EIC debt and government loans for my exact period, and good evidence that the EIC was tied tightly to the British state ("as secure for investors as the government itself," p. 388). It also shows the war years 1739-1746 pushed London prices down and forced the EIC to raise its bond rate in 1746, then prices recovered after 1749. And it gives me a dated primary source quote (Course of the Exchange, 21 March 1746) and some period books (Massie 1750, Pinto in French).
