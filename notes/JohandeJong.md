## What the source is about

This is a history of technology thesis, not a business history. It covers the whole 1700s through four case studies: ship design (the 1742 standard ships), health on board, a later ship design fight (1760s to 1780), and charts. The author says he will "touch on political issues, economic considerations and organizational problems" but technology is the main focus (p. 3 [PDF 15]). So most of it is about ships and crews. For my paper it is really strong on how sailors and soldiers were treated and how many died, which is my ethics section. It also has some useful bits on VOC profits and on how the VOC differed from the English company.

## Facts I can use

### How the VOC was set up (for "how they differed")
- The VOC had state powers in Asia. It could make treaties, build forts, "employ an army, to wage war, and to rule its territories" (p. 6 [PDF 18]).
- The stadholder got a cut: "In 1674, the VOC decided that the stadholder should receive three percent of all dividends." (p. 7 [PDF 19])
- Big difference with the EIC: the VOC built its own ships "in contrast to the English East India Company that rented its ships from private ship-owners" (p. 47 [PDF 59]; also p. 211 note 497 [PDF 223]).
- The VOC did not count wear on its ships as a cost: "the VOC did also not consider the depreciation of the value of the ships when calculating profits." (p. 217 [PDF 229]) This means VOC profit numbers might look better than they really were.

### The VOC's fortunes, 1725 to 1750
- Peak timing: the VOC was "reaching the height of its trading, shipping and shipbuilding operations in the middle of that century." (p. 7 [PDF 19]) So 1725 to 1750 is not yet the collapse.
- Ship problems before 1742: changes to the standard ship design for shallow Dutch ports had made ships worse at sea. "Storms at the Cape of Good Hope and in the Indian Ocean however made it clear that ... it had compromised the seaworthiness of the retourschepen." (p. 37 [PDF 49]) The States of Holland and the States-General got involved because the VOC monopoly "had to be renegotiated at the time" (p. 37 to 38 [PDF 49 to 50]).
- In 1740 the Heeren XVII had to answer "a scathing critique of the States of Holland on the seaworthiness of the retourschepen" (p. 181 note 407 [PDF 193]).
- The fix came from England. In 1742 the English shipwright Charles Bentam designed three new standard ships for the VOC (p. 38 [PDF 50]). The design was used from 1742 to 1795 (p. 41 [PDF 53]). "from 1742 onwards the VOC embarked again on an impressive program of renewal of its fleet" (p. 212 [PDF 224]).
- Governor-General Van Imhoff, who helped bring in the 1742 designs, wrote with more design changes on 31 October 1748, and the Heeren XVII adopted most of them on 22 September 1749 (p. 47 to 48 [PDF 59 to 60]).
- Homebound risk: "the number of ships that perished on the voyage home was at least twice the number of the ships wrecked on the outward voyage." (p. 61 [PDF 73]) That matters for an investor because the homebound ships carried the cargo.
- Value of a voyage: "Upon arrival in patria, the value of the cargo was almost three times as valuable as the amount of bullion, coins and other goods shipped out." (p. 63 [PDF 75])

### Profits and competition (including with the English)
- From the end of the 1600s the VOC had to compete with other Europeans, lost part of the intra-Asian trade, and "many VOC officials in the East were liable to corruption and involved in private trade" (p. 211 to 212 [PDF 223 to 224]).
- Pepper was stuck. Steensgaard says the Heeren XVII put Dutch and English pepper demand together at "7.2 million pond" a year and that the two companies' pepper imports hardly changed until the middle of the 1700s (p. 220 [PDF 232]). VOC pepper shipments stayed around "4.5 million pond a year" (p. 222 [PDF 234]).
- De Vries and Van der Woude argue the new goods (textiles, tea, coffee) were less profitable than spices, "causing a decline in profits for the VOC" (p. 220 [PDF 232]). De Jong thinks this is too simple.
- Value per ton of cargo, from the DAS data: Batavia cargo fell from 350 to 119 guilders per ton between 1730 and 1795. Canton (China) cargo stayed around 500 to 600 (p. 219 [PDF 231]). 1730 was the first year a ship sailed straight from Canton to the Republic (p. 219 [PDF 231]).
- After my period, the direct tea trade from 1757 made about 94% gross profit on average (Liu, quoted p. 223 [PDF 235]).
- Ships: in the 1790s VOC staff in Canton chose to go home on English East India Company ships because of the "disagreeable and lengthy voyages in our ships" (p. 91 [PDF 103]).

### Crews: recruitment, treatment, death (ethics section)
- Normal death rate going out was 7% to 15%, against 4% expected at home. Half to two thirds of those who stayed in Asia died before they could come back (p. 58 notes 119 and 120 [PDF 70]).
- "the VOC ... recruited half a million sailors and soldiers from the rural districts of the Dutch republic and half a million foreigners." Only 19 to 30% of soldiers and 40 to 60% of ordinary seamen came back. Officers came back at 70 to 80% (p. 58 to 59 [PDF 70 to 71]).
- My exact period was bad: decades with high death rates were "1690-1700, 1730- 1750 and 1760-1780" (p. 84 [PDF 96]). "Another peak in mortality figures occurred between 1730 and 1750 when the mortality for the whole voyage amounted on average to 13% and for the first part of the journey to 9%" (p. 86 [PDF 98]). The diseases were scurvy and "rotkoorts" (putrid fever, probably typhus) (p. 80, 87 [PDF 92, 99]).
- The VOC's response in my period: a new surgeons' order in 1733, a new medicine list in 1736, an expanded order in 1739 that added Peruvian bark (p. 141 to 142 [PDF 153 to 154]). Ventilators were ordered on 3 November 1746 (p. 129 note 284 [PDF 141]), and De Jong says "the VOC was the first company to install mechanical ventilators on board of (all of) its ships." (p. 132 [PDF 144]). So the VOC did try to fix things.
- The author's view on how the company saw its men: "The common crew (sailors and soldiers) were characterized as “dispensable” because a certain death rate on the way out and within the East Indies had been calculated." (p. 324 [PDF 336]) And: "Rather cynically, we might say that the death toll ... was rather convenient in this respect." (p. 65 [PDF 77])
- Soul-sellers: "volkhouders" took a recruit's bounty (two months' wages) and a "transportbrief" worth at least six months' wages, gave poor food and lodging, then sold the papers on. This is where "zielverkopers (soul-sellers)" comes from (p. 82 note 159 [PDF 94]).
- Violence: ex VOC surgeon Schuurman wrote in 1777 that VOC officers ran ships with "horrible swearing, the foulest and most despicable terms of abuse and sometimes undeserved beatings" and that it was worse than on other nations' ships (p. 91 [PDF 103]). He also said other nations (he names the English, Danes and Swedes as good seamen) lost fewer men because they had enough skilled local sailors (p. 90 to 91 [PDF 102 to 103]).
- Local people: the author says local people in Asia were "subjected to VOC’s relentless quest for profit" (p. 5 [PDF 17]).

## Gaps
- No share prices, no dividend figures, no year by year profit table. Nothing to answer "best year to sell".
- Nothing on the Batavia massacre of 1740, the War of the Austrian Succession, Madras 1746, or slavery inside the VOC. (Slave ships only come up for the WIC on p. 98 [PDF 110], and a slave owned by a VOC mate in 1772 on p. 285 [PDF 297].)
- Very little on the EIC. Only a few comparisons (ship renting, faster ships, fewer deaths).
- Most detail is on the 1770s, not 1725 to 1750.

## How useful is this source? (my take)
Medium to high for ethics, low for the money question. It is a careful thesis with tons of archive references, and the crew death numbers for 1730 to 1750 fit my time period exactly. It gives me some "how they differed" points (VOC built its own ships, no depreciation, the 1742 crisis and fix, worse treatment of crews than English ships). But it has no prices or dividends, and it says nothing on 1740 Batavia or the wars. Its best value might be the Dutch primary sources it points to, especially the Generale Missiven and the 1777 Bataafsch Genootschap essays.
