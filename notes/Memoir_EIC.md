**Citation:** East India Company. *Memoir on the Affairs of the East-India Company.* London: Printed by J. L. Cox, February 1830.
**Type:** Primary source (published by the company side, 1830), English. Anonymous. No author's name appears anywhere in it, and library catalogs list the author as "East India Company." So I cite it as a corporate author.
**Page numbers below are the printed page numbers in the book.** Printed page = PDF page minus 2 (for example printed p. 7 = PDF p. 9). The title page and the opening "1830" note have no printed numbers, so I give those as PDF pages.

## What the source is about

The title page says "February 1830" (PDF p. 1). The opening note explains why it was written: "A SELECT COMMITTEE has been appointed by each of the Houses of Parliament, " to enquire into the " present State of the Affairs of the East-India Corn- 's pany [sic, OCR]" (PDF p. 3). The EIC charter was due for renewal in 1833, and this is the company's defence of itself before Parliament decided.

Part I is a history of the company since "its union in 1708" and Part II covers the 1830 accounts (p. 3). Only the start of Part I (pp. 7 to 27) covers my period. The "FIRST PERIOD" runs "From the Union of the two Companies in 1708 to the Year 1744" (p. 7) and the "SECOND PERIOD" runs "From 1745 to 1765" (p. 13). Most of the 223 pages are about 1767 to 1830 and are not useful to me.

It is written to make the company look good. It claims the dividend "was only equal to the legal interest of money" (p. 134).

## Facts I can use

### Origins and the union
- "The East-India Company, under the title of the " London Company/ ' was incorporated by charter granted by Queen Eliza- beth on the 30th December 1600." (p. 4)
- "In 1698 the necessities of the state led to a loan from the Public of .§£2,000,000 [OCR for £2,000,000] at eight per cent., and the subscribers were incorporated by charter into a society, called the " English Company/ '" (p. 4)
- "In 1708 the London and English Companies were united, since which their title has been " The United Company of Merchants of England trading to the East-Indies."" (p. 5)
- This shows the main difference from the VOC for my paper: the EIC was born out of a loan to the English government. Its capital was basically a debt owed by the state.

### Charter extensions and loans to the government (the summary table, p. 12)
The best thing in the book is the "SUMMARY" table on p. 12. The OCR missed it, so I read it off the scan. Rows:
- 1698, 9 and 10 William III: charter to 29 September 1711, "On lending £2,000,000, at eight per cent."
- 1707, 6 Anne: extended to 25 March 1726, "On lending £1,200,000, at five per cent.and agreeing to receive only five per cent. on the two millions."
- 1712, 10 Anne: extended to 25 March 1733, "No further terms."
- 1729, 3 Geo. II: extended to 25 March 1766, plus a right to be a corporate body forever, "giving £200,000, by the 24th December 1730, and agreeing to receive from the 29th September 1730, only £128,000, or four per cent. on the £3,200,000."
- 1744, 17 Geo. II: extended to 25 March 1780, "On lending £1,000,000, by the 29th September 1744, at three per cent., the £3,200,000 to continue at four per cent."
- Bottom line: "Total Loan to Government, £4,200,000."

Note: the text on p. 7 dates the first extension to 1708 ("for fifteen years, viz. until 1723") and says the £1,200,000 was lent "without interest". The table says 1707, to 1726, at five per cent. The book contradicts itself here.

### The 1729/1730 fight and the 1730 Act
- On 26 February 1729 merchants offered £3,200,000 "to redeem the fund and trade of the East-India Company" and open the trade by licence (pp. 8 to 9).
- "The petition was rejected by 223 to 138" (p. 9). A second petition on 9 April 1730 "was nega- tived by 177 to 77" (p. 9).
- The government's offer in March: "The United East-India Company to abate out of their present fund of £150,000 per annum, £32,000 per annum" and pay "The further sum of £200,000" with no interest (p. 10). [The scan really says £150,000, but p. 11 says the old figure was £160,000, and 160,000 minus 32,000 is 128,000. So £150,000 looks like a printing error.]
- "The Act of the 3d Geo. II., cap. 14, was accordingly passed, by which the Company gave to the Public the sum of ^200,000 [OCR], and reduced the interest on the ,§£3,200,000 due from the Public from five to four per cent. viz. ^£128,000, per annum, instead of ^£1 60,000 per annum." (p. 11)
- So for my investor: in 1730 the company's guaranteed income from the government dropped by £32,000 a year, and it paid £200,000 to keep its monopoly.

### The 1744 loan
- "In January 1744 it was intimated to the Court of Directors, that if the Company would advance and lend to His Majesty, for the service of the Government, the sum of one million at three per cent., it might be the means of procuring the prolongation of the Company's term in the exclusive trade to the year 1780" (p. 11).
- The company was "to be empowered to borrow the said million on their bonds" (p. 11). So the company borrowed money to lend it to the state.
- "The 17th George II. cap. 17, was accordingly passed." It "secured to the Company the exclusive trade for the prospective term of thirty- six years from 1744." (p. 11)

### Interest cuts after 1748
- Footnote to the table: "In 1749 the Company consented to a reduction in the interest on the £4,200,000 from four to three per cent." (p. 12)
- In the 1755 dividend discussion the interest is said to have been "reduced at Christmas 1750 to three and a halfper cent. [sic], and from Christmas next reduced to three per cent." (p. 27)

### War with France, Madras, Aix-la-Chapelle
- "France having joined Spain in the war against Great Britain, the effects were soon felt in India. The French succeeded in reducing Madras, by which a loss of a£ 180,000 [OCR for £180,000] was entailed upon the Company. This settlement was restored to the Company in 1 749 by the treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle." (p. 13)
- In 1744 the directors told Bengal that "they grudged l{ no expense necessary for the just defence of the Company's " settlements.""

### Dividends
- There is NO table of dividends for 1725 to 1750. The only early figure is indirect.
- In November 1755 the directors recommended "a reduction of dividend on their capital stock from eight to six per cent." (p. 26). So the dividend was 8 per cent just before 1755. The book does not say how long it had been 8 per cent.
- Reasons given (pp. 26 to 27): more foreign nations in the India trade had raised prices; "The additional duty of five per cent, laid upon dry goods by the Act of the 21st George II." (that is 1747/48, during my selling window); the interest cut on the government debt; and wars in India stirred up "by the French under the name of auxiliaries". The directors first thought a cut of "one per cent" might do (p. 27).
- Later the directors praised the proprietors who "generously, and without a murmur" agreed "to reduce their dividend twenty per cent." (p. 20). 8 to 6 is not a 20 per cent cut, so this may refer to a different cut, or it means 2 out of 10. I need to check.

### Capital stock
- The capital in my period was £3,200,000. A 1781 deal allowed "a dividend of eight per cent, on their capital of ££3,200,000 [OCR]" (p. 54).

### Ethics
- Not much for 1725 to 1750. Later material shows the company getting rights to "booty and plunder" (p. 13) and "the inhuman incarceration of one hundred and forty- six Euro- peans in the Black Hole at Calcutta" (p. 13). It never discusses harm to Indians in my period.

## Gaps
- Nothing on the VOC except passing mentions. The 1712 petition says the company needed time "to compete with the Dutch" (p. 8), and there are "late transactions with the Dutch in Bengal" in 1762 (p. 22).
- No stock prices at all for 1725 to 1750.
- No year by year dividend list. Only the "eight to six per cent" cut in 1755 (p. 26).
- No trade figures (ships, cargo values, sales) for my period.

## How useful is this source? (my take)

Useful for the legal and money side of the EIC, but it is NOT a period primary source for 1725 to 1750. It was printed in February 1830, about 80 to 100 years after my period. It is a primary source for how the company described its own history in 1830, when it was fighting to keep its charter. So it is biased in the company's favour.

Still, the p. 12 table is very handy. It lays out every charter deal from 1698 to 1744 with the loan and interest terms. For my argument it shows that much of an EIC share was backed by a debt owed by the British government (£3,200,000 at 4% after 1730, plus £1,000,000 at 3% from 1744). That is a big difference from the VOC. It also shows real risks: the 1729 to 1730 attempt to take away the monopoly, the £180,000 loss at Madras (p. 13), and the interest cuts in 1749 and 1750 (pp. 12, 27). That suggests selling before 1749 to 1750 might be smarter. I should back up each fact with a real 1700s source, like the Acts listed above.
