## What the source is about

This is the English introduction to the printed inventory of the VOC archive in The Hague. The preface is signed "The Hague, 17th May 1992 Joyce Pennings Remco Raben" (p. 2). The archive "covers about 1277 meters" (p. 1). Chapter 1 "The Organization of the VOC" is by F.S. Gaastra (p. 3), chapter 3 "Notes on the Use of the VOC Archives" by B.J. Slot, M.C.J.C. van Hoof and F. Lequin (p. 40). Other chapters cover the history of the archive, other archives, maps, and a bibliography (p. 22, 62, 73, 110). For my paper the useful parts are chapter 1 (how the VOC was run, shareholders, dividends, decline) and chapter 3 (which records exist for 1725-1750, like dividend ledgers and the generale missiven).

## Facts I can use

### VOC structure: chambers and the Heren XVII
- Six chambers: Amsterdam, Zeeland (Middelburg), Delft, Rotterdam, Hoorn and Enkhuizen. Amsterdam got "half of all operations, Zeeland a quarter" and the four small ones "one-sixteenth each." (p. 5)
- The board of seventeen: "Amsterdam would be represented by eight directors, Zeeland by four and the smaller chambers would have one each, while the seventeenth member would be appointed in turn by one of the chambers other than Amsterdam." (p. 5)
- Gaastra's own verdict: "All in all, the central management was badly constructed. The Heren XVII did not meet in permanent session. The composition of the assembly was constantly changing, and the Heren XVII had no administrative staff." (p. 13) But he also says it was still "a force to be reckoned with." (p. 13)
- No central accounts: "The Company had no central book-keeping. Each year the six Chambers prepared a separate balance sheet." Because of this "it is difficult to get an insight into the real financial position of the chambers and of the profitability of Company business as a whole." (p. 49) Also "No profit and loss account was made up." (p. 58-59) This matters for an investor: nobody really knew how well the VOC was doing.

### Shareholders' rights (very weak)
- The 1602 charter promised money back after ten years and quick dividends, but "this provision was more honoured in the breach than in the observance by the directors." "The original investment remained unchanged throughout the entire life of the Company." The States General "supported the directors in their non-observance." (p. 6)
- "Directorship was for life. Shareholders had no influence at all on the appointment of new directors." (p. 6) Directors had to own at least f. 6000 in shares (f. 3000 in Hoorn and Enkhuizen). (p. 7)
- After 1623 "principal shareholders" (at least f. 6000) got small rights: inspecting accounts, helping nominate directors, and sending nine delegates to the Heren XVII who could only "make recommendations." (p. 8-9)

### Shares and dividends
- A share was "called an actie, had no uniform nominal value and was always negotiable: it could be sold, exchanged or used as collateral." Transfers happened "before the book-keeper in the presence of one or two directors." (p. 57)
- "Each chamber paid out dividends on the basis of the capital subscribed to that chamber." The rate "was determined by the Heren XVII either every year or every couple of years. Payment occasionally took place in the form of spices." In 1679 dividends were paid in bonds for the first time. (p. 57)
- Dividends were often decided in the spring "even before the auction of spices had taken place in March, and, furthermore, before the end of the financial year." (p. 11) So dividends were not strictly tied to actual profit.
- To pay shareholders in spring the VOC "was forced to borrow money" short term "bij anticipatie," counting on later auction sales. (p. 58) This is a warning sign about its finances.

### Relationship with the state
- At charter renewals "in wartime the States General sometimes asked for money or naval support," but "in general the Company enjoyed the support of the States General and the government strictly maintained the monopoly of the VOC." (p. 6)
- **1749 Orangist change:** "Then, following a proposal from principal main shareholders of the VOC, the stadhouder, William IV, was appointed opperbewindhebber (director-general). It was now the prerogative of the stadhouder to make a choice of new directors." But "neither William IV nor his successor, William V, took any direct interest in the administration." (p. 9) So the 1747-49 revolution did not really change how the VOC was run. Note 9 points to Isaac de Pinto's account of the 1748-49 "Stadhoudérat des Indes" (p. 21).

### Finances and decline in the 1700s
- The EIC only became "a really potent rival" at the end of the 1600s and "would cut the ground from under the feet of the VOC in various regions in the course of the eighteenth century." But the VOC "remained the largest of the Asiatic companies" until 1800. (p. 3)
- Key quote for decline: "year after year throughout the eighteenth century the Asian business of the VOC made a loss." Japan trade "almost dried up; after 1700." (p. 3)
- Home sales still covered costs, "but the financial reserves were shrinking." (p. 4)
- Yet the business got bigger: about 1700 ships in the 1600s and "a good 3000 in the eighteenth." Sales of return goods were f. 577 million in 1640-1700 and "f. 1633" (million) in 1700-1795. (p. 4)
- The crash came later: after 1780 "the Company lost its credit in one fell swoop," was nationalized in 1796, and the charter ended 1 January 1800. (p. 4)

### Violence and forced monopoly
- Spice monopolies came from "costly military operations." Banda was conquered in 1622 and the VOC concentrated cloves on Ambon "By extirpating the clove trees on various islands in the Moluccas." (p. 3)
- On so-called contracts: "The exclusive contracts had for the most part been exacted by violence so that for example, in the islands in the Moluccas it would be more correct to speak of a conquest rather than trade on a contractual basis." (p. 16)

### Records for 1725-1750 and whether they are published
- **Dividend ledgers:** Amsterdam "1628-1796 (inv. nrs. 7068-7081)," Zeeland "1710-1747 and 1760-1800 (inv. nrs. 13800 and 13806)," Enkhuizen 1720-1802. (p. 57) These cover my whole period.
- **Share transfer registers** exist for all chambers but "begin about 1750." (p. 57) So no transfer records for a 1725 purchase, except Hoorn.
- **Generale missiven** (yearly reports from Batavia to the Heren XVII) are in inv. nrs. 1053-3987 (p. 46). They give "a general report on shipping movements and trade," then each post, then "financial and staff matters." (p. 47) Published by Coolhaas and Van Goor, which "At the moment at which this introduction was being written ... covers the period up to 1737." (p. 60)
- Some records are gone: in 1821/1822 "some 9,500 to 10,000 volumes, mainly dating from the seventeenth century" were sold. (p. 29)

## Gaps (things this source does not tell me)
- No VOC share prices or dividend percentages for any year.
- Nothing on the 1740 Chinese massacre in Batavia. "Chinese" only shows up for the China Committee and book titles (p. 13, p. 117-118).
- No word "slave" or "slavery" anywhere in the text I could search. It mentions expeditions "to Madagascar and Rio Delagoa" (p. 46) but does not say what they were for.
- Nothing on the War of the Austrian Succession.

## How useful is this source? (my take)
High for the VOC side of "how the companies differed." Gaastra clearly explains the chambers, the Heren XVII, and how little power shareholders had. The 1749 stadholder point and the "made a loss" quote (p. 3) fit my question directly. It is reliable, written by the national archive and an expert historian. But it has no prices, nothing on 1740 or the war, and nothing on slavery. Its biggest value is pointing me to Dutch primary sources: the dividend ledgers, the printed *Generale missiven*, Van Dam, and De Pinto.
