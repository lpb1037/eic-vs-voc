# Notes (snippets only): Adam Smith, *The Wealth of Nations* (1776)

**File:** sources/text/Smith_1776_Wealth_of_Nations.txt
**Where I found it:** Project Gutenberg, https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3300 (I found it myself after ChatGPT could not find primary sources).
**Citation:** Smith, Adam. *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations.* London: W. Strahan and T. Cadell, 1776.
**Type:** PRIMARY source, English. Written about 26 to 50 years after my window (1725 to 1750), so it is a near-period source, not an eyewitness one. It is a famous critique of joint-stock companies.
**How I cite it:** Gutenberg has no page numbers, so I cite Book, Chapter, Part.
**How I used it:** I searched for a few key words (Dutch East India, dividend, negligence, spiceries) and read only those passages. Quotes are copied exactly from the Gutenberg text.

## Snippets

1. **Book IV, ch. 7, part 3. The Dutch and the spice islands.** "The English and Dutch companies, though they have established no considerable colonies, except the two above mentioned, have both made considerable conquests in the East Indies. But in the manner in which they both govern their new subjects, the natural genius of an exclusive company has shewn itself most distinctly. In the spice islands, the Dutch are said to burn all the spiceries which a fertile season produces, beyond what they expect to dispose of in Europe with such a profit as they think sufficient." He adds that a "savage policy" has "almost completely extirpated" the wild clove and nutmeg trees. Note Smith says "are said to" and "it is said," so he is repeating reports, not proving them. This lines up with the VOC's own letters about uprooting clove trees (notes/generale_missiven.md, Deel 11 p. 28).

2. **Book V, ch. 1, part 3, art. 1. Why joint-stock companies are badly run.** "Negligence and profusion, therefore, must always prevail, more or less, in the management of the affairs of such a company." He says such companies "have seldom been able to maintain the competition against private adventurers" without an exclusive privilege.

3. **Same place. The English company's history in my period.** "From 1708, or at least from 1711, this company, being delivered from all competitors, and fully established in the monopoly of the English commerce to the East Indies, carried on a successful trade, and from their profits, made annually a moderate dividend to their proprietors. During the French war, which began in 1741, the ambition of Mr Dupleix, the French governor of Pondicherry, involved them in the wars of the Carnatic ... they at last lost Madras, at that time their principal settlement in India. It was restored to them by the treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle."
   - Why it matters: this is a near-period account of the EIC having a steady dividend and a monopoly after 1708, then losing and regaining Madras in the 1740s war. It matches the EIC Memoir (notes/Memoir_EIC.md, p. 13) on Madras.

4. **Same place. EIC dividend level.** Before 1767 the company "gradually augmented their dividend from about six to ten per cent." That puts the dividend at about 6 percent in the 1750s and 1760s, which matches the Memoir saying it was cut from 8 to 6 percent in 1755 (notes/Memoir_EIC.md, p. 26).

5. **Book V, ch. 1, part 3, art. 1. The company's servants.** The company's monopoly exists "to enable the company to support the negligence, profusion, and malversation of their own servants, whose disorderly conduct seldom allows the dividend of the company to exceed the ordinary rate of profit in trades which are altogether free." This is Smith's view that the EIC's high costs came from its own servants. It's an opinion, and it matches Karak's finding that servants' private trade hurt the company (notes/karak-2024.md).

## Gaps
- Smith gives no share prices and no year by year numbers for 1725 to 1750.
- He is a critic of monopolies, so he is biased against both companies.
- I only read five short passages. Smith writes much more on the companies.

## How useful is this source?
Good for a quotable primary source that compares both companies and says the English one made a "moderate dividend" in my window, and that the Dutch spice policy was harsh. Weak for investment numbers.
