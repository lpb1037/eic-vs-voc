## What the source is about

This is a paper about how the VOC handled its capital. The main question is whether the VOC kept and reinvested its profits ("permanent capital") or paid everything out like the older one-voyage companies. Most of the paper (pp. 12-37) is about the very early years, 1595 to about 1610, with long tables from the Amsterdam chamber's ledgers. That part is not useful for me. The part I need is Tables 8 and 9 (pp. 40-42) and the discussion on p. 43. These give the VOC's assets, debts, equity, profits and dividends every ten years from 1640 to 1801, so I get numbers for 1720, 1730, 1740 and 1750. The paper barely mentions the EIC and has nothing on ethics.

A warning about quality: the author is listed with an email and ORCID but no university (p. 1), and the paper still has drafting notes left in it, like "see tables # and" (p. 33) and "Provide a translation for stucken" (p. 16, footnote 10). So I should check its numbers against De Korte (1984) or another published source before leaning on them.

## Facts I can use

### How the VOC was set up (difference from the EIC)
- The VOC was "chartered in 1602" and both Robertson and Dari-Mattiacci et al. call it "the first company to have a permanent capital." (p. 7)
- The EIC came first but was slower to get permanent capital: "The EEIC was chartered in 1600, however didn’t have a permanent capital until 1657" (p. 7, footnote 4). The paper says the EIC copied the VOC: the "English East India Company (EEIC) adopted a permanent capital because of the success of the VOC" (p. 7).
- The paid-in capital stayed the same for the whole history of the company: ƒ6,449,588 in every column of Table 8, 1640 to 1801 (pp. 40-42). So VOC dividends quoted as a percent are a percent of this fixed nominal capital.
- The VOC also borrowed with bonds from the start. "As early as 13 August 1602, the VOC issued bonds to raise funds." (p. 31)

### VOC investors could not see the books
- "The VOC kept its internal finances secret, meaning that the EEIC only had share prices, dividends, and the VOC's physical presence in the East Indies as a reference." (pp. 7-8)
- Even the official company history by the lawyer Pieter van Dam (asked for in 1693) "was meant to function as a reference for and only for the Heren XVII as it was kept a secret" (p. 9).
- The home balance sheets only covered assets in the Netherlands. "the summary and general statements only include domestically held assets." Assets in Asia were kept in separate books by the Boekhouder Generaal Batavia. (p. 37)
- What this means for me: a 1725 investor in the VOC was buying mostly on trust and on the dividend record. They could not check if the company was actually making money.

### Profits and dividends by decade (Table 9, p. 41)

| | 1720-1730 | 1730-1740 | 1740-1750 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Retained earnings | 10,001,034 | (9,858,417) | (15,038,554) |
| Distributed earnings (dividends) | 14,242,840 | 13,060,416 | 11,448,019 |
| Total earnings | 24,243,874 | 3,201,999 | (3,590,535) |

- So in the 1730s the VOC earned only about ƒ3.2 million in total but paid out about ƒ13.1 million. In the 1740s it had a loss of about ƒ3.6 million and still paid about ƒ11.4 million in dividends. (p. 41)
- My own math from the cumulative dividend percents (p. 41): about 221% of capital paid out 1720-1730, 202.5% in 1730-1740, and 177.5% in 1740-1750. That works out to roughly 22%, 20% and 18% of nominal capital per year on average. Dividends were still high but going down.
- The weird retention ratios for these decades (-307.88% and 418.84%, p. 41) are just because earnings were tiny or negative. They don't mean much on their own.

### The paper's summary of the decline (p. 43)
- For the earlier period: "During the period when the VOC reinvested its profits (1640-1730), it did so at a retention ratio of ~27%. This coincided with the expansion of the VOC’s assets in the East Indies (figure 10)." (p. 43)
- For my period: "From 1730-1780, the VOC remained profitable, but these profits were not reinvested. Unlike during the prior century, holdings in the East Indies decreased substantially." (p. 43)
- The end: "From 1780-1800, the company suffered a net loss of ƒ90 million, and liabilities increased from ƒ26 million to ƒ136 million. By 1801, the debt to asset ratio was ~240%, the VOC was insolvent and dissolved." (p. 43)
- Problem: the text says the VOC "remained profitable" from 1730, but its own Table 9 shows a loss for 1740-1750 (p. 41). I should trust the table over the summary sentence, or check De Korte.
- What this means for me: 1730 looks like the turning point. My 1725 investor bought near the peak of VOC equity. During 1725-1750 the VOC kept paying big dividends, but it was paying them out of past savings and new debt, not current profit. That is a sign of a company in decline even if shareholders were still getting paid.

### Small details on violence and war (early period only)
- The first fleet's revenue included the "Santa Catharina, a Portuguese carrack (large ship) captured by the VOC" and "The VOC was allowed to retain captured merchandise from privateering." (p. 18, footnote 14)
- The first fleet's costs included "Cost of artillery and ammunition for war" (p. 19, Table 3.1).
- These are from 1603-1604, not my period. They only show that armed force and seizing ships were part of the VOC's business from the start.

## Gaps

- No share prices at all, for either company.
- Only ten-year snapshots, so I can't tell which year between 1745 and 1750 was best to sell from this paper.
- Almost nothing on the EIC except its charter and capital dates (p. 7).
- Nothing on the War of the Austrian Succession, Madras 1746, Aix-la-Chapelle 1748, or the Batavia massacre of 1740. The paper does not explain why profits collapsed after 1730.
- Nothing on slavery, forced labor, or ethics.
- Not peer reviewed, and has unfinished parts (p. 16, p. 33). Its p. 43 summary does not fully match its own table.

## How useful is this source? (my take)

Medium. It has the one thing I could not find elsewhere so far: a decade by decade picture of VOC profits, dividends and debt right across 1720-1750. The pattern is clear. The VOC was strongest around 1730, then earned very little in the 1730s, lost money in the 1740s, and kept paying large dividends by running down its equity and doubling its home debt. That supports an argument that the VOC was a worse long-term bet than it looked in 1725. But it is an unpublished paper with rough edges, it has no share prices, no EIC comparison, and no ethics. I should use it for the trend and back up the exact numbers with De Korte (1984) and Willemsen (2004).
