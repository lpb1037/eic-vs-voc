## What the source is about

A narrow technical paper. The authors found extra issues of the Dutch newspaper *Amsterdamsche Courant* and used them to settle whether the Amsterdam prices for British stocks (Bank of England and EIC) before 1747 were cash ("spot") or forward ("time") prices. Answer: spot. It is NOT about the VOC and doesn't tell the story of the EIC. But it does have a few real EIC prices from the 1720s and 1730s (in a small table of data errors), plus the size of EIC dividends in 1724-1741. So it adds a little that my other sources don't.

## Facts I can use

### Actual EIC prices (Amsterdam, in £) from Table 9 (p. 84)

| 6/6/1725 | 167 | 154.5 | 163.5 | 164.5 |
| 5/11/1733 | 161 | 151 | 161 | 160 |

- What this tells me: EIC was about **£148-149 in October 1724**, three months before my ancestor buys in January 1725. By **June 1725 it was about £163.5-167**. So a January 1725 buy was probably somewhere around £150 (my guess between those two dates, not a real quote).
- In 1733 about £160-161, in 1735 about £148-150, in September 1739 (right before the war) about £154-156.

### Average EIC prices
- "The average Dutch share price of BOE and EIC during our sample is about £140 and £160, respectively." (p. 71) The sample here is 1724-1741.
- For 1747-1794: "The average stock price for BOE and EIC over this time period was about 137 and 170, respectively." (p. 73)

### EIC dividends 1724-1741
- "For both the Bank of England and the East India Company, dividends were paid out twice a year, albeit at different times for the separate stocks. The dates were common knowledge and occurred at the same time every year." (p. 66)
- "The dividends for the Bank of England for this period were either 3 or 2.75 pounds, and for the East India Company, the dividends were either 4 or 3.5 pounds." (p. 77)
- "the actual size of the dividend payments varied little." (p. 77, footnote 8)
- Timing: "In the case of the East India Company, the ex-dividend periods began in the middle of January and July for the years 1724 and 1725. From 1726 on the ex-dividend periods started at the end of January and July" (p. 71, Fig. 7 caption).
- My own math (not from the paper): two payments of £4 = £8 a year, or two of £3.50 = £7 a year. At the average price of about £160 that is a yield of roughly 4.4% to 5% a year. Over 20+ years that adds up to more than the price change, which matches what Golez and Koudijs say about dividends being most of the return.
- Timing tip for the paper: since the EIC went ex-dividend in January, buying in January 1725 right before vs. right after the ex-dividend date would change whether the ancestor gets that half-year dividend.

### How the Amsterdam market worked
- New sources: "numerous additional issues of the Amsterdamsche Courant were located by the authors, covering the years 1724-1727, 1730-1737, and 1740-1741." (p. 62)
- Van Dillen's data gave "on average, two observations per month" and Neal ended up with "only 1679 joint observations" for 1723-1794 (p. 62). The new set has "1,117 and 1,118 observations for the two stocks" for those years (p. 63).
- Before 1747 Amsterdam quotes were spot: "the prices of stock in the Bank of England and the East India Company traded in Amsterdam before 1747 were almost certainly reported as spot, not time." (p. 83) After 1747 they were time (forward) prices (p. 65). So Amsterdam EIC prices after 1747 run slightly higher than London (p. 73, Table 4 on p. 76).
- Van Dillen's own words (quoted p. 74-75): comparing the newspaper "with those found in brokers' notes preserved from 1725 to 1737 it appears that in that period the quotations are cash prices. In the year 1747 both prices are sometimes mentioned."
- The settlement ("rescontre") dates for British stocks in Amsterdam "were adapted from trade in shares of the VOC and occurred on the 15 of February, May, August, and November of each year." (p. 65) A small link between the two companies' markets.
- Dutch time trading "was very popular and in part due to the ability to use shares as collateral for short-term loans (Dickson 1967, p. 335)." (p. 65)
- Dividends showed up in Amsterdam prices later than London: "The lag appears to be around 30-40 days in each case." (p. 77)
- Data accuracy: they found 67 wrong London prices in Neal's data out of "over 4,100 days with recorded prices" (p. 84) and 81 differences in the Amsterdam prices (p. 63). The appendix says 86 (p. 83), so the paper is a bit inconsistent there. Most errors were under £1.

## Gaps
- No VOC prices or anything about the VOC beyond the settlement dates.
- No EIC prices for 1745-1750 at all, which is the sell window I care most about. Their new data stops at 1741.
- Only six EIC price points, and only because they were next to typos. Figure 1 (p. 64) graphs Bank of England prices 1724-1727, not EIC.
- Nothing about wars, events or company profits.

## How useful is this source? (my take)
Low to medium. It's mostly about a technical data question. But it's the only one of my three sources that gives actual EIC price numbers near my buy date (about £148-149 in October 1724, about £163.5-167 in June 1725) and the actual dividend sizes (£4 or £3.50 twice a year). Those are useful for working out what the ancestor paid and earned. It says nothing about 1745-1750.
