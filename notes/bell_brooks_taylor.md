**Citation:** Bell, Adrian R., Chris Brooks, and Nick Taylor. "Time-varying Price Discovery in the Eighteenth Century: Empirical Evidence from the London and Amsterdam Stock Markets." *Cliometrica* 10, no. 1 (2016): 5-30. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11698-014-0120-z
**Type:** Secondary source (modern economics journal article), English. This is the accepted manuscript, not the final printed version.
**Page numbers below are the manuscript's own page numbers** (page 1 starts on PDF page 3, so PDF page = manuscript page + 2). They will NOT match the published journal pages 5-30.

## What the source is about

A finance paper testing whether London or Amsterdam "found" the price first for two British stocks traded in both cities, the Bank of England (BOE) and the EIC, from August 1723 to December 1794. Most of it is math. But section 3 (pp. 5-7) has a short history of events that moved prices, facts about Dutch investors owning EIC stock, and Figure 1 (p. 19), a graph of EIC prices from 1723 on. There is **no VOC data** in it and no table of EIC prices, so any EIC numbers for 1725-1750 have to be read off the graph.

## Facts I can use

### EIC prices 1723-1750 (Figure 1b, p. 19)
- The authors only describe the price trend in words for after 1760: "For the EIC, there is no real upward drift in prices. There was a rise of over 100% from the lowest value of £117 in May 1762 to around £280 by April 1769. But almost the entire increase was wiped out in the early 1770s." (p. 6)
- "there are some fairly long swings lasting perhaps a decade at a time." (p. 6)
- **My rough eyeballing of Figure 1b (price in £, London and Amsterdam lines almost on top of each other). These are NOT exact:**
  - Starts around 128 in late 1723, about 150 by early 1725 (when my ancestor buys).
  - Up to about 175 around late 1725 or 1726, then a sharp drop to about 130 around 1727.
  - Climbs to its early high of about 200 around 1731-1732.
  - Falls hard to about 135 around 1734, recovers to about 185 around 1737.
  - About 150 to 160 around 1740-1741 (start of the war).
  - Jumps to about 200 around 1743.
  - Drops to about 155 to 160 around 1745-1746 (fits the Jacobite panic, see below).
  - Back up to about 185 to 190 by about 1748-1750, and close to 200 by the early 1750s.
- What this might mean for me (my guess from the graph): buying at about 150 in early 1725 and selling in 1748-1750 (after the war ended) looks better than selling in 1745-1746. Need real numbers from Neal or ICPSR to confirm.
- The EIC was riskier: "the EIC was the considerably more volatile of the stocks, tying in with its reputation for being involved in inherently riskier businesses." (p. 6) Also "the relatively calm markets enjoyed in the 1750s" (p. 6).

### Events 1725-1750 that affected prices
- War of the Austrian Succession: "the first major conflict was the War of Austrian Succession (1739-48), in which Britain was later allied with the Dutch republic and Austria against France, Prussia and Spain. The theatre of war involved conflict with France in North America and India." (p. 6)
- Exact war dates the paper uses (from Neal 1987): peace "09/08/23 - 19/10/39", war "21/10/39 - 23/10/48", peace again "11/11/48 - 14/07/56" (pp. 9-10, footnote 20). So the war ended in late October 1748.
- The 1745 Jacobite rising (the paper calls it "Jacobin"): it "caused an associated financial panic. The Jacobin forces were able to invade from Scotland and penetrate as far as Derbyshire, before retreating and being defeated at Culloden in April 1746." (p. 6) This is a big reason NOT to sell in late 1745.
- "Despite all of this conflict, it is of note that the London market remained open to foreign investors throughout the period of study and therefore Amsterdam residents were continually able to trade." (p. 6)
- Barnard's Act, 1733: "sought to ban trading in futures, although it is not clear that the Act was successful" (pp. 5-6). It "forbade options and forwards being traded in London" (p. 2, footnote 4).
- War news: during wars "relevant information from a battlefield on the European continent may reach Amsterdam before it reaches London" (p. 7). Their results back this up: London's share of price discovery was lower in wartime (p. 9).
- South Sea Company stock was "very thinly traded after the bubble of 1720" (p. 5, footnote 11).

### Dutch ownership of the EIC (useful for "how the companies differed")
- "These British companies were attractive to Dutch investors as they provided more potential growth opportunities than those available for their own domestic stocks." (p. 2) Big point for me: Dutch investors themselves were putting money into the EIC instead of only the VOC.
- "In the early 1700s, Dutch investors have been argued to have held up to a third of BOE's stock (Bowen, 1989), although over the century, interest gradually shifted towards the EIC so that by 1767, the Dutch owned 30% of the EIC." (p. 2)
- "Wilson (1941) argues that demand from Amsterdam helped to support stock prices at times when it was lacking from England." (p. 2)
- Dutch traders were later "blamed as a contributory factor in causing instability and excessive volatility of the share prices." (p. 2)
- Cross-listing "was almost certainly the first example of cross-listing anywhere in the world." (p. 2)

### How the data and dividends worked
- Data: ICPSR 1008, "Course of the Exchange, London, 1698-1823 and Amsterdamsche Beurs, Amsterdam, 1723-1794" (p. 5, footnote 13). London prices from John Castaing's list "printed twice per week from 1698" (p. 5). Amsterdam prices only "transcribed every two weeks by van Dillen (1931)" (p. 5).
- Dividends: "the effect of dividends on prices occurs in London around the ex-dividend date, but affects Amsterdam prices around 30 days later." (p. 7)
- Amsterdam prices "were spot prices prior to 1747 and time prices thereafter (Beach et al., 2013)" (p. 6).
- News crossed by "mail packet boats, which sailed at best twice per week" (p. 10).

## Gaps
- No actual EIC price numbers for 1723-1760 in the text, only the graph. No dividend amounts (they used Gary Shea's dividend data but don't print it, p. 5).
- Nothing on the VOC at all.
- Nothing about Madras falling to the French in 1746, or EIC profits.

## How useful is this source? (my take)
Medium. It has no price table, but it's the best of my three sources for the events between 1739 and 1748 (war, Jacobite panic, Culloden), and the graph gives a rough shape of EIC prices that suggests selling after the war (1748-1750) rather than during the 1745 panic. The Dutch ownership facts are also great for the "how they differed" part, since even Dutch investors preferred the EIC. Downside: it's mostly about the 1760s onward when it talks about prices.
