## What the source is about

This article is about the women who did household work in the Dutch (VOC) and English (EIC) settlements in Bengal in the early 1700s. Many of them were enslaved. Others were freed slaves or "free Christian" and "Portuguese" women who ran taverns, lodging houses and brothels, or worked as housekeepers. The VOC headquarters was Chinsura (part of Hugli) and the EIC headquarters was Calcutta (p. 74). Most of the examples fall between about 1712 and 1755, so it lines up well with my 1725-1750 window. It is not about trade or profits at all. Its value for me is the ethics section: it proves that officials of BOTH companies, including top VOC directors, owned, bought, sold and punished slaves in Bengal during my period.

## Facts I can use

### Slavery existed in both company towns
- The abstract says the article covers "female household workers in the Dutch and English East India Company (VOC and EIC, respectively) ports in Bengal in the early eighteenth century." (p. 71)
- Where the slaves came from: "from Mando, Makassar, Banda, and Batavia in the present-day Indonesian archipelago, from Ceylon, Patna, and Bengal" and these were places "where they were captured and sold into slavery." (p. 72) Batavia was the VOC's Asian capital, so the slave supply ran partly through VOC networks.
- Who owned slaves: "English, Dutch, Armenian, Greek, and Portuguese householders owned slaves." (p. 74)
- What they did: "The enslaved population in the settlements, both male and female, primarily performed the work of social reproduction, as nurses, cooks, housekeepers, and sex workers, in houses and taverns." (p. 74)
- Sexual labour was the main work: "Providing sexual services formed the foremost work of the Portuguese or free Christian women of the settlements." (p. 82)

### Top VOC officials bought and sold people (dates inside my window)
- November 1740: junior merchant Harmanus von Blom sold two slaves, Febrauraij van Bengale and Valentijn van Bengale, "to the VOC director Jan Sichterman." The sale paper said they were both "good cooks". (p. 76, source note 9: 1.11.06.11/1715, fo. 85)
- 1743: Anna de Rosario "earned thirty-five Arcot rupees by selling her slave Sabina van Falta to the assistant merchant, Jan van Hoorn". (p. 91) An assistant merchant was a VOC rank.
- 1753: a Portuguese woman from Calcutta sold her slave in Chinsura "for seventy Arcot rupees". (p. 91)
- The author's summary: "slaves were capital investments." (p. 91)
- 1755: "On the eve of his departure in 1755, Jan Kerseboom, Director of the VOC in Bengal, manumitted fifteen such families". (p. 78) So the VOC director in Bengal owned at least fifteen enslaved families. The list is in Table 1. (pp. 79-80)

### Manumission (freeing slaves) and how "freedom" was limited
- "Manumission of enslaved workers was common in the settlements." (p. 76)
- Table 1 lists VOC manumissions in 1732, 1734, 1740 and 1755. (pp. 79-80) One 1734 entry says the owners "Retained right to recall manumission". (p. 79)
- Freedom was often delayed. In 1736 EIC ship captain George Penuse freed his slaves but said they were to "serve as before". (p. 77)
- "It was thus not a mere slip of the tongue when the terms "servant" and "slave" were used interchangeably." (p. 77)
- Conclusion: "manumission did not translate into freedom from domestic labour." (p. 93)
- Some freed women got money. Leonara van Mandhar nursed VOC Director Roger Bereneert and he left her 10,000 rupees (p. 82). She later used 1,550 of it "to emancipate seven of her slaves." (p. 91)

### Violence and punishment in VOC settlements
- 1712: a slave woman in Chinsura was caught planning to poison her master. "After she confessed, the Hugli council of the VOC hanged her." (p. 86, source VOC 8742, fo. 527)
- 19 November 1739: September van Mandhar, a slave of a junior merchant, killed his master. "He was apprehended and sentenced to the rack. Upon his death, his body was to be thrown into the Ganga." (p. 86, source VOC 8787, fos 1132-1133)

### Violence and punishment in EIC settlements
- 10 October 1719: two EIC sailors broke into a house and raped two girls, a nine year old slave named Sabina and a five year old named Biviana. They got "thirty-nine lashes by rattan". (p. 86)
- The author says "This shows that in EIC settlements rape was considered a minor offense compared to petty theft, which was often punished by death." (p. 86)
- May 1728 (right after my January 1725 investment): an enslaved woman, Hanna, stole ornaments worth one hundred rupees from her Armenian master in Calcutta and ran away with her lover Hackema, a freedman. They were caught the next day and "were both sentenced to death" by the Mayor's Court. Hanna said she was pregnant, but "she was judged unworthy of life." (p. 87)
- They had fled across the river to the old Mughal thana, which "signified the boundary of the EIC's zamindari, beyond which point they had no legal jurisdiction." (p. 87)
- Calcutta court bias: in the Rosa de Rosario inheritance case (proceedings of February 1746/47), the author says "the evidence and interests of company men were given undue importance over the voices of the housekeepers." (p. 85)

### Dutch vs English differences (small, but some)
- Town layout: in Chinsura VOC officials lived alongside Portuguese, Armenians, Greeks, Banians and Muslim merchants, but in Calcutta "the native merchants were pushed to the north of the city". (p. 74) The English town was more segregated.
- Calcutta was more of a power base: "By the mid-eighteenth century, the settlement around the fort in Calcutta had taken the form of a commercial, administrative, and military centre." (p. 74)
- The EIC got zamindari rights over three villages in 1698, which became Calcutta. (p. 74)
- Marriage: "In 1688, the EIC allowed their men in India to marry Roman Catholic women in the settlements. Such marriages were also allowed in the Dutch settlements." (p. 85)
- My take: the article does NOT say one company was kinder than the other. Both towns had slavery, sale of people and harsh punishment. The VOC evidence shows directors themselves owning slaves. The EIC evidence shows death sentences for slaves who ran away and stole.

### Other useful 1725-1750 details
- Domingo Ash, a Portuguese woman in Calcutta, was "the most important supplier of arak to the EIC ships for at least forty years." Her licence ran from 1704 "until 1743/4". (p. 81)
- An Augustinian friar counted "25,000 Christian Portuguese in Bengal" in the early 1700s. (p. 77, note 21)
- After the EIC's expansion following 1757 and Buxar (1764), officers "no longer had to rely on their Indian Ocean networks to supply their domestic labour force." (p. 93)

## Gaps (things this source does not tell me)
- Nothing on share prices, dividends, profits, or trade volumes.
- No numbers on how many slaves each company or its staff held in total. It is examples, not statistics.
- Most of the slave owners are company employees acting privately. The article does not say if the companies themselves (as institutions) owned slaves in Bengal. That matters for the ethics question.
- Nothing about weavers, merchants, or the nawabs.
- Nothing on VOC slavery in Batavia or the Cape, which is a much bigger part of the VOC story.

## How useful is this source? (my take)
Very useful for the ethics part, not useful for the money part. It is peer reviewed and every example has an archive reference, so I trust it. The biggest point for my paper: an "ethical investor" could not escape slavery by picking one company. A VOC director bought slaves in 1740 (p. 76) and another VOC director owned fifteen enslaved families in 1755 (p. 78). The VOC council hanged one slave and broke another on the rack (p. 86). On the EIC side, the Calcutta court sentenced a runaway enslaved woman to death in 1728 (p. 87) and treated rape lightly (p. 86). It also gives me Dutch archive references that count as non-English primary sources.
