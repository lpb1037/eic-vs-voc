## What the paper is about

Neal compares prices of the same English stocks (Bank of England, East India Company, South Sea Company) on the London and Amsterdam exchanges from 1723 to 1794. He finds the two markets moved almost perfectly together and were "efficient," meaning you couldn't easily predict price moves. The paper is about how the markets worked, NOT about which company was a better investment. **It only covers the English EIC, not the Dutch VOC.** It doesn't print a list of EIC prices by year either.

## Facts I can use

### My ancestor could buy EIC stock from either city
- English company shares "were traded simultaneously on the stock exchanges of London and Amsterdam at the end of 1723 and continued to be traded through peace, war, and revolution into the nineteenth century." (p. 9)
- The charters of the big English companies "permitted foreigners to own shares and this right was upheld by the Crown and the companies despite occasional challenges from members of Parliament." (p. 12)
- Prices in the two cities were almost the same. The correlation of EIC price levels between London and Amsterdam was 0.993 for 1723-1794. (Table 1, p. 37)
- So in January 1725 my ancestor could buy EIC stock in London or Amsterdam at about the same price. The choice of company matters, not the city.

### Dutch investors were piling into the EIC during my exact period
- This is the most useful fact in the paper for me. "Dickson found that Dutch holdings of Bank of England stock rose from 10.5% of the total capital in 1723-4 to 30.3% in 1750 while their share of East India Company stock rose from 13.4% to 21.4% over the same period." (p. 31)
- Neal says 1723 to 1750 "was precisely the period when the Dutch built up their holdings in the English joint stock companies and long term government debt most rapidly." (p. 31)
- Total foreign holdings in the "Big Three" English companies went from 9.3% of capital in 1723-24 to 19.2% in 1750. (p. 17)
- Dutch buying of Bank of England stock had a "Dutch rush" between 1721 and 1726. (p. 16)
- Why this matters: Dutch money was moving INTO English stocks during 1725-1750. Dutch investors themselves seemed to think the English companies were a better place for their money. That's a hint for my recommendation, but I need actual returns to back it up. Neal doesn't say why the Dutch did this, and "the more interesting questions of the ebb and flow of Dutch investments ... remain unanswered." (p. 18)

### EIC paid higher dividends than the Bank of England
- The Amsterdam vs London price gap "was 1.6 points for Bank stock and 2.5 points for East India stock. This reflects the generally higher dividend rates paid by the East Company stock." (p. 28)
- Dividends were paid twice a year: "dividends were paid semi-annually." (p. 27) So my ancestor would have collected about 40 EIC dividend payments between 1725 and 1745.

### Investors were optimistic in the early period
- For 1723-1737 Neal reads his results as showing "optimistic expectations by investors during this period." (p. 28) That's when my ancestor would be buying.

### The War of the Austrian Succession (1739-1748) is the one weird period
- Neal dates the war period from October 19, 1739 (War of Jenkins' Ear) to October 1748 (Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle). (p. 15)
- The only time EIC prices in Amsterdam show an odd pattern is "1739-1748, the War of the Austrian Succession." (p. 21)
- In his conclusion: the normal patterns were "sometimes masked by unusual expectations (e.g., during the War of the Austrian Succession)" and "the War of the Austrian Succession remains an anomaly." (p. 34)
- Why this matters: my cash out window (1745-1750) overlaps the end of this war (1748) and the peace right after. The war years were unusual for EIC prices. I need actual prices to see whether selling during the war (1745-47) or after the peace (1748-50) was better. My guess (NOT proven yet) is that prices recovered after the 1748 peace.

### Markets were generally efficient
- "efficient markets for the leading British financial securities appear to be in place in both Amsterdam and London after the South Sea Bubble of 1720." (p. 22)
- For the EIC in London, "only in the London market for East India stock that we find consistent evidence in each sub-period for an efficient market." (p. 20)
- What this means for my paper: in 1725 nobody could have predicted the price swings. Only someone with knowledge of the future (like my time traveling letter!) could time the sale.

### Barnard's Act (1734)
- "in 1734, Barnard's Act (7 Geo. II, cap. 8) forbade all dealings in options and future deliveries of stocks, with a fine of 500 pounds." (p. 22-23) It was "persistently violated in fact." (p. 23) Probably just background. It means London deals were mostly cash deals.

### How to read prices from the period
- London prices from Castaing's *Course of the Exchange* were cash prices. Amsterdam prices were forward (future delivery) prices, which ran a little higher. (p. 22-24)
- London quoted a stock "ex dividend" (without the upcoming dividend) for about two weeks before each payment. Amsterdam always quoted "with dividend." (p. 28-29) **If I compare prices from different sources I need to keep this in mind.**
- Prices are quoted as a percent of the share's face value. Example from a 1730 contract: Bank of England stock bought "at a price of a Hundred and Forty Five and a Quarter per Cent." (p. 29)

### Actual period document quoted (primary source inside a secondary source)
- A Dutch contract from 4 April 1730 between Jacob Reynst and David Leeuw of Amsterdam, buying "One Thousand Pounds Sterling Capital Shares of the Bank of England at London." (p. 29, translated by Dickson 1967, p. 335). It's not the EIC, but it's a real example of a Dutch person buying English stock. Good color for the letter.

### VOC shares were very expensive
- In Amsterdam, small fractions of VOC shares called "ducaton" shares were created to let smaller investors in "since the cash value of each original share had increased over the decades to very high levels indeed." (p. 24-25) My ancestor might need a lot of money to buy one full VOC share. Need to check VOC share prices in 1725.
- You couldn't legally own a VOC share until the transfer was written in the company's books, which only happened when the books opened for dividends. (p. 24)

## Gaps (what this paper does NOT tell me)
- No VOC returns or fortunes at all. Only that VOC prices were printed in the *Amsterdamsche Courant* from July 14, 1723. (p. 14)
- No actual EIC prices for 1725 or 1745-1750, only correlations and regressions.
- Nothing about ethics, slavery, or what the companies did in Asia.
- It's from 1985, so it's older research. Neal's 1990 book *The Rise of Financial Capitalism* is the updated version, and that's what Golez and Koudijs used for their data.

## How useful is this source? (my take)

It also tells me the 1739-1748 war years were unusual for EIC prices, which matters for when to cash out. It doesn't help with the VOC side at all.
