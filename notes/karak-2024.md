## What the source is about

This article asks whether there was a "politics of commerce" inside Bengali society, or only fights between the EIC and the nawabs. Most of it (pp. 39-52) is a long case study of a 1778 dispute over market taxes in Calcutta between two rich landowners, Nabakrishna Deb and Madan Dutt, and the small shopkeepers and street sellers caught between them. That is after my period. But the background sections (pp. 34-37 and pp. 53-56) cover the EIC in Bengal from 1717 to 1765: dastaks (duty free passes), private trade by company servants, fights with the nawabs, and Plassey. It says nothing about the VOC.

## Facts I can use

### The 1717 farman and dastaks (the basic EIC privilege in my period)
- Dastaks were "passes allowing duty-free trade". They "were issued to the company as per the terms of the farman (royal order) received by the EIC from Mughal emperor Farrukh Siyar in 1717." (p. 34)
- The dispute: "Although the farman allowed duty-free trade, the nawabs of Bengal insisted that only the external trade of the company and not the internal trade of its servants and free merchants would be exempt." (p. 34)
- EIC servants and free merchants "sought to make private fortunes in the internal trade of Bengal by evading taxes due to the Bengal nawabs. They did so sometimes by force and sometimes by plying their trade in the company's name by using the EIC's dastaks". (p. 34) This is Peter Marshall's view, which Karak summarizes.
- Important for investors: "the company itself did not have serious stakes in Bengal's internal trade until the mid-1760s." (p. 34) So the dastak abuse made money for EIC employees, not for EIC shareholders.

### EIC vs its own servants, 1717-1757
- "Between 1717 and 1757, whenever the Bengal nawabs objected to the allegedly illegal sale of dastaks to Indian merchants by British private traders, high-ranking company officials in both London and Bengal supported the nawab." (p. 53)
- Why: "The company deplored the sale of its dearly bought dastaks to Indian merchants, since that did not enhance the EIC's monopoly profits." (p. 53)
- So "the interests of private traders within the EIC, let alone those of free merchants outside the company, never coincided with the monopoly interests of the EIC." (p. 53)
- Most settlements gave in. "On the eve of the Battle of Plassey in 1757, it was only Calcutta that continued to hold firm on the issues of free inland trade, the sale of dastaks, and the shielding of the nawab's subjects against oppression and extortion." (p. 53) This led Siraj-ud-Daulah to sack Calcutta in 1756. (pp. 53-54)

### EIC and British politics, 1720s-1750s (exactly my period)
- After 1714 a Whig oligarchy ruled Britain, and "the EIC propped up this system with its monopoly profits." (p. 53)
- "From the 1720s to the 1750s, the EIC acted as a bulwark of the oligarchic order, and precisely during this period, the total tonnage of British private trading in Bengal tripled." (p. 53, citing Marshall, *East Indian Fortunes*, p. 58)
- My take: the EIC in 1725-1750 was tied closely to the British government. That probably made it a safer, more protected investment, but the big growth in Bengal trade was private trade by its servants, not company profit.

### Was the dastak system pure abuse? (a different view)
- Karak follows Spencer Leonard here. "a large proportion of the trade covered by the allegedly illegal sale of dastaks was only nominally European". (p. 36)
- "Numerous Indian and Asian merchants benefited by plying their trade under the English flag". (p. 37)
- He admits "British private traders benefited from what was essentially a protection racket". (p. 37)
- But local merchants chose it because they "preferred to pay a flat rate that ensured the regularity and security of their private property, rather than having to pay a multitude of arbitrary tolls and taxes to multiple claimants". (p. 37)
- For my ethics section: this is a counterpoint. Not all dastak trade was the EIC forcing itself on Bengal. Some Indian merchants wanted English protection.

### Conditions in Bengal 1725-1750
- Bengal's economy "was quite vibrant during this period as compared to other regions of the subcontinent". (p. 53) Good for both companies trading there.
- But with the Mughal empire breaking up, each regional kingdom charged its own duties, which was "especially harmful for Bengal's trade with upper India." (p. 53)
- "within Bengal, the nawab's officers often charged three, five, or even ten times more than the established duties at toll stations along riverine trade routes." (p. 53)
- The Maratha raids: "When the Marathas plundered and raided Bengal in the 1740s, no Hindu zamindar in Bengal showed any sympathy for them on religious grounds." (p. 63) The raids happened during my 1745-1750 selling window, which might have hurt trade.

### Who the EIC traded with
- "an Anglo-Bania commercial order did exist in Bengal between roughly 1700 and 1765." (p. 63)
- Dadni merchants were "those who took advances from the European companies and then acquired raw silk, piece goods and calicoes from the interior". (p. 63) Rila Mukherjee found many castes "had worked for the EIC between 1701 and 1750." (p. 63) This is the advance system both companies used to buy cloth.

### Coercion and extraction (mostly after 1757)
- Marshall "acknowledges the increased hardships faced by peasants, weavers and consumers alike under the company regime". (p. 35)
- "The colonial state that was established in 1765 was territorial and extractive, and it had major adverse effects on eastern India." (p. 36)
- After 1765 "monopoly farms on key commodities such as salt, betelnut and tobacco were created in 1765" and "the revenue collected in Bengal was used to pay for the EIC's investment". (p. 56)
- "In effect, trade was made free for a monopoly company, and private trade was absorbed within the monopoly system." (p. 56)
- By late 1769 "Bengal was teetering on the brink of a devastating famine." (pp. 56-57)
- The "drain began as early as 1760" when Mir Kasim gave the EIC revenue rights to Burdwan, Midnapur and Chittagong. (p. 64, note 133)
- Clive himself complained about "a combination between the blacks and whites" to "divide all the revenues of the Company between them". (p. 64)
- My take: the worst EIC coercion in Bengal came after my investor has sold. But the roots (dastaks, private trade, fights with the nawab) were growing in 1725-1750.

## Gaps (things this source does not tell me)
- No mention of the VOC at all. Can't use it for comparison.
- No EIC profit, dividend, or share price numbers.
- The 1725-1750 parts are short background summaries based on other books. The main archival research is all about 1778.
- No specific events in Bengal between 1725 and 1750 other than the Maratha raids and the general dastak conflict.
- No details on coercing weavers, just one line (p. 35).

## How useful is this source? (my take)
Low to medium. It is a strong, recent, peer reviewed article, but it is mostly about 1778 and caste in Calcutta markets, which is outside my question. The useful part is about two pages of background (pp. 34, 53) on dastaks and private trade from 1717 to 1757. The key point for my paper is that the dastak abuse helped EIC servants get rich but did not help EIC shareholders, and the company in London actually sided with the nawab against its own servants (p. 53). It also gives me a counterpoint for the ethics section: Indian merchants sometimes wanted English protection (p. 37). Its best value is pointing me to Bhattacharya (1954) for EIC Bengal trade 1704-1740.
