## What the source is about

This is a big picture economics paper about whether banks and stock markets caused economic growth, with case studies of Amsterdam (1640-1794), England (1720-1850), the US and Meiji Japan. It uses VOC year-end share prices (from Neal's reading of the Amsterdamsche Courant, plus van Dillen 1931) and EIC share prices from Neal (1990), but only as inputs to statistics (a "Q" ratio in VAR models). **It does NOT print a single VOC or EIC share price, dividend, voyage count or cargo value as a number.** I checked every table (Tables 1, 2, 4 on pp. 41, 43, 45) and they are only regression coefficients. The only thing close to real data for my years is Figure 1 (p. 52), which I can only eyeball. So this source adds little on prices. It is useful for one line contrasting the two companies and for pointing to data sets.

## Facts I can use

### The VOC price data (where it comes from)
- "Neal (1990) has improved upon van Dillen's (1931) share price series for the VOC from 1723 through 1794." (p. 5)
- How the yearly price is picked: "I use the final price observation in each year for VOC shares from Neal's reading of the Amsterdam Courant. These observations are usually from the last week in December. I use the final price observations from van Dillen (1931) for years that are unavailable in Neal's data." (p. 5, footnote 3)
- The data is online: "The VOC prices and other stock market data from Neal (1990) are available on the world-wide web from the Inter-University Consortium for Political and Social Research (ICPSR)" (p. 5, footnote 3). This is the ICPSR data set I should try to get.
- "1723 and 1794, which is the period when continuous annual prices of VOC shares become available" (p. 11)
- Important for reading VOC prices: "The VOC did not change its share capital over the 71-year period that I consider so that the Q of VOC equity is the ratio of price to par value of the shares." (p. 11, footnote 8) So VOC prices are quoted against par, and I don't have to worry about splits or new shares for the VOC like I do for the EIC.

### VOC trade around 1720-1750 (Figure 1, p. 52)
- Text: "the market value of VOC trade, hard money exports by the VOC, and the size of drawing account balances at the BA all saw dramatic increases following the financial crisis of 1672 until the mid-1720's, increases that were sustained until the decline of the VOC after 1780." (p. 6)
- Figure 1 is in "mil. of 1790 fl." as three-year moving averages (p. 6 and p. 52). **Rough eyeballing only, not exact numbers:**
  - VOC trade (market value of returning cargoes): peaks around 24 million around 1725-1727, slides down to about 16 million around 1741-1742, back up to roughly 18 by about 1745 and about 22 by about 1750.
  - VOC hard money exports: peak about 9 million around 1727, low around 3 in the late 1730s, back to about 6 to 7 by 1750.
  - VOC debt at the Bank of Amsterdam: jumps to about 7 to 8 million around 1742, then falls.
- What this might mean for me (my guess): the VOC's trade peaked right about when my ancestor buys (1725) and was weaker in the late 1730s and early 1740s. This fits the idea that the VOC was past its best.
- Sources of the trade data: number of voyages from NHDA "Data Set D0100 titled 'Dutch-Asiatic shipping, 1602-1795'", money shipments from "F3503", market value of trade from "F3505 titled 'Returning ships and products, 1641-1796.'" (p. 5, footnote 4)

### Differences between the companies
- The best line in the paper for my thesis. The EIC data "cover the heyday of the EIC", while the VOC data "covered the period of gradual decline for the Dutch enterprise." (pp. 18-19)
- VOC: "held a monopoly by statute and in practice on Asiatic trade east of the Cape of Good Hope" (p. 3). Capitalized "with 2,167 shares at a par value of 3,000" florins (p. 3, citing Glamann 1958).
- The Bank of Amsterdam "made large loans to the VOC and to the government over the next two centuries" (p. 4).
- EIC: created "a permanent capital of £369,891 in 1657" and expanded "early in the 18th century following a merger in 1708" (p. 18).
- "The EIC's capital was small compared to the turnover of its operations, and as such it depended heavily on short-term debt and internally-generated funds to get voyages out to sea." (p. 18)
- The Bank of England "made over ninety loans to the East India Company between 1709 and 1744" (p. 16).
- Neither company was short of money. VOC: "This is not the type of behavior that one would expect from a company that was having trouble raising cash" (p. 10). EIC: "financing constraints did not bind for the EIC over this period" (p. 19, Table 4 covers 1710-1745).

### Background for England
- "the real value of international trade ... rose by 50 percent between 1720 and 1760" (p. 13).
- EIC prices: "Due to the painstaking work of Larry Neal (1990, pp. 231-257), we now have a nearly complete picture of EIC share price from this point onward." (p. 19)

## Gaps

- No EIC prices either. Nothing on 1745-1750 events (war, Jacobites, Madras).

## How useful is this source? (my take)
Low to medium. It does not give the prices I hoped for, so I can't use it to pick a sell date. But it is a respected Fed conference paper, and the "heyday of the EIC" vs "gradual decline" of the VOC line backs up picking the EIC. It also confirms VOC capital never changed, so VOC prices are easy to compare across years. Mostly it sends me to ICPSR, Neal and van Dillen.
