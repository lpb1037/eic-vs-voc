## What the paper is about

This is a finance paper, not a history of the companies. The authors build one long stock market data set from 1629 to 2015 and test whether the dividend yield (dividend divided by price) predicts future returns. For 1629 to 1812 their "market" is a portfolio of Dutch and English company shares, and both the VOC and the EIC are in it. So the paper is mostly useful for its data sources and some background facts. It does NOT give a year by year price table for the EIC or the VOC in the text, so I can't pull 1725 vs 1745-1750 prices straight from it.

## Facts I can use

### Both companies traded in the same market
- "Amsterdam was arguably the most important financial center of the 17th and 18th century. It was closely integrated with the market in London and featured trade in the largest English securities (Neal 1990)." (p. 5)
- English shares were traded in Amsterdam "but not the other way around." (p. 49) So a Dutch investor could buy EIC shares, but an English investor would have a harder time buying VOC shares.
- Pound to guilder exchange rate risk was small: "Since both countries were on metallic standards, exchange rate fluctuations were relatively small with an annual standard deviation of 3%." (p. 5)
- Shareholders in both had limited liability. (p. 5, p. 44)

### The VOC
- "The VOC was the world's first publicly traded corporation. It was founded in 1602, and its capital became permanent in 1613." It "held the Dutch monopoly on trade with Asia" and the "Dutch government nationalized the company in 1796." (p. 44)
- It was huge. Even in 1696, after the EIC and Bank of England are added, "the VOC still accounts for 63% of total market capitalization." (p. 6)
- Dividends were irregular early on. "Depending on which shareholder group dominated in a particular year, dividends were either very high or foregone altogether (Van Lent and Sgourev, 2013)." (p. 6) This is about the 1600s, so I need to check whether VOC dividends were steady by 1725.

### The EIC
- "The EIC started in 1657 and held the English monopoly on trade with Asia. Around 1700 the trade on Asia was opened up, and in 1708 the government allowed the 'old' EIC to merge with its main competitor, the 'new' EIC, to restore its monopoly." (p. 46)
- The Bank of England "was an important lender to the EIC as well." (p. 46) This shows the EIC was tied closely to the British government and its finances.
- The English companies "have a complicated history of capital calls, rights issues, repurchases, stock dividends and share splits." (p. 48) If I compare EIC prices over 20 years I need to watch for this.

### Context for 1725
- The South Sea Bubble was in 1720. The South Sea Company "accounted for 61% of total market capitalization" that year, then "After the bubble burst, the company was largely liquidated; in 1733, it constituted only 6% of our index." (p. 46) So my ancestor is buying in January 1725, just a few years after a huge crash. Prices may still be recovering.
- Market size compared to the economy peaked "64% (during the 1720s)" of the GDP of Holland. (p. 6-7) The 1720s were a big moment for stock markets.

### General return facts (whole 1629-1812 period, not company specific)
- Average nominal returns were about 6% a year in the early data. (p. 9)
- Most of the return came from dividends, not price growth. For the whole sample only "37% coming from price appreciation." (p. 9)
- Real dividend growth "In the 17th and 18th centuries ... are below 1%." (p. 10)
- What this means for me: in this era you mostly made money by collecting dividends, not by selling at a high price. So the total return for my ancestor = dividends collected 1725 to the sale + the sale price minus the buy price. The dividends from 20 years might matter more than the exact year they sell.

### The main finding, and how it might help with timing
- High dividend yields (low prices compared to dividends) come before periods of high returns, and low yields come before low returns. "the dividend-to-price ratio tends to increase before a period of high returns." (p. 11)
- Yields and returns "tend to increase in recessions." (p. 11)
- For my question: if one company's price was high compared to its dividend in 1725, that points to lower returns afterward. I'd need the actual dividend yields for 1725 to use this.

## Gaps (things this paper does not tell me)
- No actual EIC or VOC share prices for 1725 or 1745-1750 are written out in the text. The figures are for the whole market, not each company.
- Nothing about the War of the Austrian Succession, Madras, Batavia 1740, or anything on ethics. (It does mention the WIC and the Royal African Company were in the slave trade, p. 44 and p. 47, but neither is the EIC or VOC.)
- The risk free rate data has "a gap ... between 1720 and 1727." (p. 9)

## How useful is this source? (my take)
Medium. It's a good source for background facts (both companies, the 1720 bubble, dividends being most of the return) and it's a reliable academic paper. But it does not answer "which company did better 1725-1750" by itself. Its biggest value is pointing me to Neal (1990) and Van Dillen (1931), which should have the actual numbers, and to some Dutch primary sources I need for the bibliography rules.
